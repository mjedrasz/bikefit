# Gravel Bike Fitting — Reference Angles (Recreational Riding)

Sources: BikeFittr, BikeDynamics, BikeFitAdviser, PMC/NIH research, Colby Pearce, bicyclenest.com, graveleur.cc (2024–2026).

---

## Knee Angle

Measured at the **bottom of the pedal stroke (6 o'clock)**.

| Method | Recreational gravel range |
|---|---|
| Included angle (180° = straight leg) | 137–147° [3][12] |
| Flexion from full extension (dynamic, pedalling BDC) | 33–43° [12] |
| Flexion from full extension (static Holmes) | 25–35° [11][13] |

- Below 137°: saddle too low — knee overload, loss of power [3]
- Above 150°: saddle too high — hip rocking, hamstring strain [3]
- Saddle can be 2–3 mm lower than equivalent road setup to allow quick weight shifts over rough terrain [8][9]

**Minimum knee angle (top of stroke, 12 o'clock):** ~65° target for most riders [5]; avoid below 70°, which raises shear forces on the knee joint [3]. If too acute despite a correctly set saddle height, investigate crank length or ankle mechanics [5].

---

## Torso Angle

Measured from **horizontal** to a line from hip to shoulder, hands on hoods, cranks horizontal.

| Recreational gravel range |
|---|
| **45–55°** from horizontal [13][8] |

- 45°: more active/performance end of recreational
- 55°: relaxed, all-day comfort on rough terrain
- More upright than road (road endurance sits at 40–50° [1]; aggressive road at 35–40° [8])

---

## Hip Angle

Measured between thigh and torso at the **top of the pedal stroke (12 o'clock)**.

| Recreational gravel range |
|---|
| **55–65°** [5][13] |

- More open (larger angle) than road racing due to more upright torso
- Opening the hip angle improves breathing capacity and reduces lower back stress on long rides [1]
- Below 50°: risk of hip impingement on the upstroke; requires high flexibility [1][5]

---

## Elbow / Arm Angle

| Position | Range |
|---|---|
| Upper arm to torso (shoulder forward angle) | 80–90° [1] |
| Elbow flexion from straight | 20–30° (150–160° included) [13] |

Elbows should be soft and unlocked — absorbs trail vibration and reduces hand/shoulder fatigue.

---

## Ankle Angle

| Pedal position | Range |
|---|---|
| 3 o'clock (power phase) | ~90° neutral (85–95°) [5] |
| 6 o'clock (bottom of stroke) | 5–20° toe-down / 95–110° included [5] |
| 12 o'clock (top of stroke) | 20–30° toe-down [5] |

### Peer-reviewed research

11. **Holmes J.C., Pruitt A.L., Whalen N.J.** — "Lower extremity overuse in bicycling." *Clin Sports Med.* 1994;13(1):187–205.
    Origin of the Holmes static knee flexion method (25–35° at BDC).

12. **Bini R.R. et al.** — "Comparison of static and dynamic methods based on knee kinematics to determine optimal saddle height in cycling." *Acta Bioeng. Biomech.* 2019. PubMed: 32022807
    <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32022807/>
    Establishes that dynamic BDC angle is ~8° greater than static (Holmes), yielding a dynamic range of 33–43°.

13. **Holliday W. et al.** — "Anthropometrics, flexibility and training history as determinants for bicycle configuration." *PMC* (2022)
    <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9219349/>
    Validates 25–35° static KFA and 45–55° torso angle for recreational cyclists.
